plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))

When summing over multiple days, you convolve the original distribution with itself just like the dice scenario. The distribution changes from being very right-skewed with a mean of 2 to getting more of a bell-curve shape and a larger total count that increases by 2 with every extra day added.

#Problem 1c.

# 1. Setup Parameters & convolutions over 50 days

mu_base = 2         # Base background rate per day
n_days_large = 50   # Number of days to average over to demonstrate central limit theory

# Generate baseline Day 1 PMF (truncated at 14 where probability drops near 0)
x_base = np.arange(0, 15)
pmf_large_sum = poisson.pmf(x_base, mu_base)

# Iteratively convolve over the specified span of days
for _ in range(n_days_large - 1):
    pmf_large_sum = np.convolve(pmf_large_sum, poisson.pmf(x_base, mu_base))


# 2. Transform from sum distribution to a daily average distribution

x_sum_ticks = np.arange(0, len(pmf_large_sum))
x_avg_ticks = x_sum_ticks / n_days_large  # Convert total counts into daily averages

# Theoretical Gaussian metrics according to CLT:
# Mean of averages = base mean. Standard deviation of averages = base std_dev / sqrt(N)
clt_mean = mu_base
clt_std = np.sqrt(mu_base) / np.sqrt(n_days_large)

# Continuous x-axis range for mapping the smooth Gaussian curve
x_gaussian_curve = np.linspace(0, 4, 300)

plt.plot(x_gaussian_curve, pdf_gaussian_curve, 'r--', lw=2, label='CLT Gaussian Prediction')

When averaging days, the probability distribution smooths out and retains the mean of 2 from the count/X = 2 per day of the first distribution. The distribution is pretty symmetrical and loses the right skew that the initial distributions had. This makes sense with the central limit theorem because now the days are averaged and the distribution is no longer a Poisson distribution as it is no longer discrete. It is converging toward a Gaussian distribution with more days averaged over.

def calculate_observation_sigma(N, Y, mu_bg=2):
    
    # 1. Total expected background counts
    expected_bg = mu_bg * N
    
    # 2. Standard deviation of the background counts over N days
    sigma_bg = np.sqrt(expected_bg)
    
    # 3. Total observed counts
    observed_total = Y * N
    
    # 4. Calculate sigma (Z-score)
    sigma = (observed_total - expected_bg) / sigma_bg
    
    print(f"--- Observation Results over {N} Days ---")
    print(f"Expected Background Counts: {expected_bg}")
    print(f"Observed Total Counts:      {observed_total}")
    print(f"Significance of Discovery:  {sigma:.2f} sigma")
    
    return sigma


# N = 50 days, and assume an observed daily rate of Y = 10 gamma rays/day

Problem 3b. If I sample a Gaussian distribution 10,000 times, what is the probability that the maximum value out of those 10,000 trials at least a specific value z?

$$P\left(\max(X_1, \ldots, X_{10{,}000}) \geq z\right) = 1 - \left[\Phi\left(\frac{z-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right]^{10{,}000}$$

#Problem 4 look elsewhere effect / trials factor

sigma = 1.0      # width of background Gaussian
N = 10000        # trials factor (number of pixels)

p5 = norm.sf(5)  # one-sided 5-sigma p-value (~2.87e-7)

# A) Version 1: single known pixel
print("4a. signal needed (V1) =", norm.isf(p5) * sigma)

# B) Version 2: global p = p5, so local p = 1 - (1 - p5)^(1/N)
p_local = -np.expm1(np.log1p(-p5) / N)   
print("4b. signal needed (V2, N=10k) =", norm.isf(p_local) * sigma)

# C) Penalty for N = 10k
print("4c. extra brightness =", (norm.isf(p_local) - 5) * sigma, "sigma")

# D) Vary the trials factor over orders of magnitude
print("\n4d. N, signal needed for global 5 sigma")
for n in [1, 10, 100, 1e3, 1e4, 1e6, 1e9, 1e12]:
    pl = -np.expm1(np.log1p(-p5) / n)
    print(f"   {n:>8.0e}  {norm.isf(pl) * sigma:.3f}")

The sensitivity penalty for the trials factor is so low (1.55 sigma greater than version 1) because the tail of a Gaussian distribution falls off very quickly, so moving a small amount to the right along the x-axis reduces the probability by several orders of magnitude. A trials factor of N = 10^4 makes the background 10^4 times more likely to produce a signal-like fluctuation, so the signals need to significant enough to cancel out that factor of 10^4 from observing more pixels.

4e. With a different shaped distribution, probabilities at the tail could fall off slower or faster than a Gaussian distribution leading to a difference in the sensitivity penalty of many trials. An exponential distribution as example is skewed to the right and has a longer / larger tail than a Gaussian distribution, and so having a trials factor of N = 10^4 might have a larger impact on the sigma value than it does for the Gaussian distribution above.

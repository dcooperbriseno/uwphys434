  1. Statistical Question: "Is a given temperature reading statistically consistent with the nominal experiment temperature of 12 K given our system's known 0.4 K standard deviation, or is it an extreme outlier?
  2. In math: we will reject any point outside $ \frac{x_i-12.0}{0.4}>Z_{crit}$ where $ Z_{crit} = 5 $.

1B) The smaller the threshold is, the more data will be thrown out. If there were a loose threshold where anything beyond $|Z| > 5$ is discarded then very few data points would be omitted besides extreme outliers. If the threshold were to be decreased to $|Z| > 2.5$ or $3$ then we can say that about $1.2%$ or $0.3%$ of the data would be omitted respectively. Using the 68-95-99.7 rule, we know that at this level the amount of data that is omitted is predictable and a small amount of good data will be omitted at these thresholds.

1C) Yes, there are mistakes of commission. In the example given above, a data point at 12.7 is close to the mean. With $\sigma = 0.4$, the Z-value is $1.75$ for this point. This would be impossible to remove with thresholds, and it would be hard to discern that the data point itself is bad without having other context for what was happening when it was collected. With a dataset like this, we can say that bad data points remaining is not always avoidable but most outliers can be removed with a threshold that is large enough to not throw out a bunch of good data. (like $|Z| > 3$ or $|Z| > 6$)

  1. The question in words is: What apparent change in displacement is large enough to have a 1 in 3.5 million ($5 \sigma$) chance given that the change in x and y follow a symmetric 2D gaussian distribution?

  2. In math: The general formula for the pdf of a 2-D gaussian is the following:

$ f(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}} * \exp[-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2}(\frac{x-\mu_x}{\sigma_x^2}+\frac{y-\mu_y}{\sigma_y^2}+\frac{2\rho(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)}{\sigma_x\sigma_y})$

With $\mu_x$ and $\mu_y$ being zero since the 2D gaussian distribution is centered at (0,0), and both $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$ being 1, the mathematical representation of the probability density function simplifies to:

$ f(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}} $

To find the total probability of a stationary star randomly fluctuating outside a threshold radius $R$, we must integrate this joint density function across the entire 2D plane outside that circular boundary.

Change coordinates: We convert your Cartesian PDF into polar coordinates by substituting:

  • $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$
  • $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$

Setting up integral: We integrate the angular component $\theta$ from $0$ to $2\pi$, and the radial component $r$ from our threshold radius $R$ to infinity ($\infty$): $$\text{Probability}(r > R) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{R}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-\frac{r^2}{2}} \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$

Solving Integral: Since the integrand has no $\theta$ dependence, integrating over $d\theta$ yields $2\pi$, which cleanly cancels out the normalizing constant: $$\text{Probability}(r > R) = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi} \int_{R}^{\infty} r e^{-\frac{r^2}{2}} \, dr$$

Evaluating the remaining radial integral using $u$-substitution yields: $$\text{Probability}(r > R) = \left[ -e^{-\frac{r^2}{2}} \right]_{R}^{\infty} = 0 - \left(-e^{-\frac{R^2}{2}}\right) = e^{-\frac{R^2}{2}}$$

Matching to the $5\sigma$ threshold: To match the false alarm rate of a standard two-tailed 1D $5\sigma$ test ($5.733 \times 10^{-7}$), we solve for $R$: $$e^{-\frac{R^2}{2}} = 5.733 \times 10^{-7}$$

$$-\frac{R^2}{2} = \ln(5.733 \times 10^{-7}) \approx -14.373$$

$$R^2 = 28.746$$

$$R = \sqrt{28.746} \approx 5.36 \text{ arcseconds}$$

  1. a 5 sigma detection of motion corresponds to a change in distance of 5.36 arcseconds

Problem 3¶

If we observe for 8 hours per night for 15 days, and expect 1 cosmic ray per minute, then we predict 1 * 60 * 8 * 15 = 7200 cosmic rays over the period of 15 days of observation.

  1. The question in words is: If there were no moon in the sky, how significant or likely is our actual detection of 6800 cosmic rays over the period of 15 days given an expected amount of 7200? If this p-value is exceedingly small, we conclude that we have a statistically significant detection of the moon's presence absorbing cosmic rays.

  2. In math: Expected Background: First, we calculate our expected mean background count ($\lambda$): $$\lambda = 1 \text{ Cosmic ray/min} \times 60 \text{ min/hr} \times 8 \text{ hrs/night} \times 15 \text{ nights} = 7200 \text{ cosmic rays}$$

Probability Density Function: Because cosmic rays arrive independently and randomly in time, the true background distribution is a Poisson distribution with parameter $\lambda = 7200$: $$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$

Summation: To evaluate our observed deficit of $N = 6800$ counts, we need to find the cumulative probability of observing $6800$ or fewer events: $$\text{p-value} = P(X \le 6800) = \sum_{k=0}^{6800} \frac{7200^k e^{-7200}}{k!}$$

Central Limit Theorem Approximation: Because our expected count is very large ($\lambda = 7200 \gg 20$), the Central Limit Theorem allows us to approximate this discrete Poisson distribution with a Gaussian distribution, where:

  • $\mu = \lambda = 7200$
  • $\sigma = \sqrt{\lambda} = \sqrt{7200} \approx 84.85$

In this continuous approximation, the summation converts into a standard integral: $$\text{p-value} \approx \int_{-\infty}^{6800} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx$$

  1. Converted to sigma:

$ Z = \frac{N-\mu}{\sigma} = \frac{6800-7200}{\sqrt{7200}} \approx -4.71 $

The sigma value is -4.71, close to the 5 sigma threshold for discovery

The unique aspect to this problem is that we are looking for the absence of signal instead of a high signal like in other problems. This is because the moon should be absorbing some of the cosmic rays that we would have otherwise observed if it weren't there.

import math
from scipy.stats import norm, poisson

# 1. Define input parameters
rate_per_minute = 1
hours_per_night = 8
total_days = 15
observed_events = 6800

# 2. Calculate the total expected background (lambda)
total_minutes = hours_per_night * total_days * 60
expected_events = rate_per_minute * total_minutes  # 7200

# This calculates the exact sum of P(X = k) from k=0 to 6800
p_value_poisson = poisson.cdf(observed_events, expected_events)

mean = expected_events
std_dev = math.sqrt(expected_events)

# Z-score
z_score = (observed_events - mean) / std_dev
p_value_gauss = norm.cdf(z_score)


# 5. Print out the results
print(f"--- Cosmic Ray Moon Shadow Analysis ---")
print(f"Total Expected Background: {expected_events} counts")
print(f"Observed Background:            {observed_events} counts\n")

print(f"p-value:          {p_value_poisson:.5e}")
print(f"Significance:      {abs(z_score):.2f} sigma")
